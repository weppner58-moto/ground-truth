00 — Why this brief exists

Who I am, and why I wrote this

A percentage is what caught my attention. The agreements are what I found, and they are the reason this brief exists.

LiveWire's marketing lead posted about 386% growth in the second quarter. That number is accurate, and on its own it would be worth a paragraph — a big percentage on a small base is the oldest trick in reporting and not much of a story. What made it worth two weeks of work is what turned up once I opened the filings behind it: two contract manufacturing agreements, signed the same day, that between them make buying motorcycles value-destructive and not buying them expensive. A services agreement Harley-Davidson can cancel on ninety days' notice for no reason at all. A loan that was quietly stripped of its conversion feature and given a lien over substantially all assets. And a fourth vehicle programme under contract for two years that appears in no filing's narrative.

None of that is in the earnings release, and none of it has been reported. It is in exhibits — the documents attached to filings that almost nobody opens.

I picked LiveWire Group to start because it is the only pure-play electric motorcycle company in the United States that has to file with the SEC. Everyone else in this category — Zero, Stark, Sur-Ron, Dust before it was bought — is private, which means the only figures available are the ones they choose to publish. LiveWire has to file audited statements, disclose its contracts, and put its numbers on the record whether they flatter it or not. That makes it the one place in this industry where the announcements can be checked against the arithmetic.

And the gap is instructive. A quarter gets reported as 386% growth; the filing shows 923 motorcycles over twelve months. A market share of 76% is claimed; three consecutive annual reports never say what market. A new product is positioned as the turnaround; the manufacturing agreement behind it commits the company to buy volume it has no way to sell. None of that is hidden. It is simply in documents most people never open.

That is the hurdle I am trying to clear. Suppliers deciding whether to tool up for this category, investors sizing a position, dealers weighing a franchise, engineers choosing where to spend a career, and buyers wondering whether the company behind a $15,000 motorcycle will exist to warranty it — all of them are working from press releases because the primary documents are long, dull, and written to be technically true rather than clear.

So I read them. All 260 filings for both companies, plus the manufacturing agreements, the loan documents and the exhibits that nobody quotes. Then I show the work, link every figure to its source, and publish my corrections when I get something wrong. The goal is not to be adversarial toward LiveWire. It is to make the electric motorcycle market legible to people who have to make decisions about it — and to give them the same footing the manufacturers already have.

02 — Revenue

The business is smaller than the day it was sold

Revenue was $35.8 million in 2021, the year the merger was announced. Trailing twelve months to 30 June 2026: $31.3 million. Peak was 2022, at $46.8 million.

Five years, an accumulated deficit of $337.4 million, and the top line is below where it started — against a plan that called for 51x growth.

A product that appears in no filing except one exhibit

The Amended and Restated Contract Manufacturing Agreement filed 22 May 2026 is with KYMCO, not Harley-Davidson. Its recitals name the covered product: “LiveWire's Parisian maxi-scooter.” The parties signed a memorandum of understanding on it dated 17 October 2024, for the joint design and manufacture of an EV maxi-scooter.

The words “Parisian” and “maxi-scooter” appear nowhere else in any LiveWire filing — not in a 10-K, not in a 10-Q, not in a press release. A fourth vehicle programme, under contract for nearly two years, exists in the public record only in the preamble of an exhibit.

The agreement also commits LiveWire to a Minimum Annual Volume Commitment for each product, enforced by an Order Deficit Impact Fee: miss the volume and LiveWire must either take delivery of the shortfall or pay for it. That is a take-or-pay obligation to KYMCO sitting alongside the per-product minimums owed to Harley-Davidson from calendar 2027. Exhibit A — the products, the volumes and the prices — is redacted in full.

Each pivot moved the price. None of them moved the overhead.

Two observations follow. First, the S2 is now selling at roughly the realised average price the segment reports — $11,999 sticker against $11,483 recognised — which is what makes the net realisable value write-downs legible: the cost-plus transfer price from Harley-Davidson sits above where the market will clear the bike.

Second, the Honcho is not an extension of this ladder. It is a different business. At $4,999, built by KYMCO rather than Harley-Davidson, sold into the minimoto segment rather than the premium roadster segment, it shares a badge with the S2 and very little else. Dust points the same direction — off-road, lightweight, cheaper to homologate. Together with STACYC, the company's only profitable segment, the portfolio now describes a retreat from the premium on-road thesis that justified a $2.31 billion valuation.

Read left to right, the picture is a company that vacated the top of its own map. The LiveWire ONE, once $29,799, now sits at $13,999 and is the most expensive thing the brand sells. Can-Am — which owns the Alta patents — brackets the S2 line from below on both the road and dual-sport rows.

And the Honcho does not land in open space. It lands inside the e-moto band, against Sur-Ron and Talaria machines that already occupy that price, while sitting $1,400 above the combustion Grom that outsells everything in the category. The one genuinely uncontested position on the map is the gap between $6,000 and $10,000 — which is where nobody, including LiveWire, currently builds anything.

04 — Precedent

Alta Motors, and the first time this happened

Before LiveWire was a public company, Harley-Davidson's capital was decisive in the fate of a different American electric motorcycle manufacturer.

DateEvent
March 2018Harley-Davidson takes a minority stake in Alta Motors, the Brisbane, California maker of the Redshift electric off-road and supermoto line, in a partnership to develop and produce electric motorcycles.
Autumn 2018Approximately six months later, Harley-Davidson ends the arrangement and elects to build its own Silicon Valley R&D operation instead.

Early 2019BRP (Bombardier Recreational Products) acquires Alta's assets, including patents, stating no intention of reviving the brand.
2024–2026BRP is selling electric motorcycles under the Can-Am name. Harley-Davidson's electric business does not appear in Harley-Davidson's strategy.
Why this belongs in the file

The pattern is not that Harley-Davidson caused Alta to fail — Alta was a venture-funded startup with its own problems, and companies fail for many reasons. The pattern is narrower and better documented: Harley-Davidson's decision to stop funding was, on both occasions, the event that determined what came next.

In 2018 that decision arrived six months after the investment and Alta closed within weeks. In February 2026 it arrived in an audited annual report: “The Company does not plan to make additional investments in LiveWire beyond the amount outstanding under the Term Loan.”

The difference is that this time it is a filed statement in a public company's 10-K, with a maturity date attached and a security interest over substantially all of the borrower's assets.

There is a second-order irony worth recording. The Alta patents went to BRP, which now competes in the category. The Silicon Valley R&D centre Harley-Davidson chose to build instead became part of the LiveWire business whose R&D spend has since fallen from $54.1 million to $23.9 million a year. Eight years and roughly $394 million of cumulative losses later, the electric motorcycle assets Harley-Davidson declined to buy are being sold by a competitor, and the ones it built are not named in its own five-year plan.

05 — Unit economics

Where the money actually goes

In Q2 2026 the Electric Motorcycles segment recognised $13,655 per motorcycle in total segment revenue — $11,483 of it the motorcycle itself, the balance parts, accessories and apparel. Cost of goods sold was $23,172 per unit. Selling, administrative and engineering expense was $58,049 per unit.

What the write-down actually means

A net realisable value adjustment means LiveWire bought S2 motorcycles from Harley-Davidson and immediately marked them down to what they can be sold for. Under the Contract Manufacturing Agreement those motorcycles are priced cost-plus, with a mark-up on H-D's cost.

The write-down is arithmetic proof that the cost-plus transfer price exceeds the motorcycle's market value. The loss is real; it is recognised at purchase rather than at sale, and it originates in the contract rather than on the factory floor.

Three qualifications, stated before anyone else states them

The base is the trough. Q2 2025's 55 units is what makes 386% impressive and what makes the flat loss damning. Anyone using that quarter as a base, in either direction, has chosen a flattering comparison.

LiveWire has a legitimate explanation, in the same filing. Operating expense fell $967 thousand. Personnel costs came down $1,728 thousand on lower headcount; that saving was offset by increased S4 Honcho development spend and costs for the Dust acquisition and the ATM programme. Reinvesting a cost reduction into the next product is what a functioning company does.

The annual trend runs the other way, and it is real. See below.

The other number: 76% market share

The same post claimed 76% U.S. market share. Three things about it.

On the source of that figure

The most probable source is Motorcycle Industry Council registration data, which is the standard basis for U.S. two-wheel share. MIC data is not public. It is a paid product distributed to reporting manufacturers and subscribers, and the underlying model-level detail is available to OEMs and licensees rather than to readers.

That has a direct consequence for this brief and for anyone reading the claim: the specifics cannot be independently verified. Not because the figure is necessarily wrong, but because the dataset it most likely rests on is not one a reader can obtain, and LiveWire has published no definition alongside it. This section therefore examines what can be checked — whether a definition exists in the filings, and which measure the company itself nominates — and stops there.

First, the segment is not defined anywhere in the filings. The words “kilowatt” and “horsepower” do not appear once in LiveWire's FY2025 Form 10-K. Whatever denominator produces 76% is a marketing construction with no disclosed basis in the company's SEC reporting — which means it cannot be checked, and a share figure that cannot be checked is not a share figure.

That absence holds across three consecutive annual reports. FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025 contain no segment definition, no power threshold, and no reference to the Motorcycle Industry Council.

How a share figure of that size gets built — author's note

U.S. two-wheel share is generally measured from registration data compiled by the Motorcycle Industry Council from manufacturer reporting. A structural feature of that dataset is worth understanding: segment classification is declared by the manufacturer. Where a given model sits — dual-sport or enduro, standard or cruiser, on-road or off — is a decision the maker submits, not one an independent body assigns.

In product planning roles at Honda and Harley-Davidson, the author's direct experience is that classification is treated as what it is: a positioning decision, weighed with its effect on reported competitive position in view. That is an ordinary and well-understood part of how the industry uses this data. It is also why a share percentage drawn from it means nothing without the segment definition attached.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Restrict a segment to on-road electric motorcycles above a power threshold and the U.S. competitive set narrows to a handful of nameplates. Set that threshold at 50 kilowatts and machines making 47 horsepower — the Can-Am Pulse and Origin, the most significant recent entrants — fall outside it entirely, while LiveWire's own 63 kW and 75 kW products remain inside. The threshold, not the performance, decides who is counted.

None of this is an assertion about what LiveWire did. The company has published no definition, so no claim can be made either way. The point is narrower and sufficient: a share figure whose denominator is undisclosed cannot be verified, and the mechanism by which such a figure can be made large is standard industry practice.

Second, the company's own filing says which number measures share — and it isn't the one being quoted. From the Q2 2026 10-Q:

LiveWire Group, Form 10-Q, page 31

“Retail Motorcycle Unit Sales made through both the Company-owned dealership and Independent Retail Partners are a key measure of consumer demand and market share for LiveWire's electric motorcycles.”

One day, two filings

The 386% quarter was announced on 23 July 2026, in an 8-K under Item 2.02.

On that same day, LiveWire received notice from the New York Stock Exchange that it was out of compliance with Section 802.01C — as of the 22 July close, the average price of its common stock had been under $1.00 for thirty consecutive trading days. That notice was disclosed six days later, in a separate 8-K under Item 3.01.

The sequence, in the company's own filings

23 July — Q2 earnings release: unit sales up 386%. Same day: NYSE non-compliance notice received.
29 July — the delisting notice is disclosed by 8-K.
3 August — compliance regained; the trailing average recovers above $1.00.
4 August — the board doubles executive severance and carves an H-D acquisition of 100% of the stock out of the definition of “change in control.”
5 August — the 10-Q is filed.

The share price roughly doubled on the earnings news. Twelve days after being told it was on the delisting track, the company was off it. The 386% headline is, quite literally, what cured the deficiency.

07 — The next product

The S4 Honcho meets a category ceiling

The S4 Honcho is priced: $4,999 for the Trail, $5,499 for the Street. It is manufactured by KYMCO, not Harley-Davidson.

On the 23 July earnings call, management said production had begun and that first units would reach authorised retailers later in the summer. The Honcho therefore contributed nothing to the 386% quarter, and nothing to the trailing twelve months analysed throughout this brief. Everything below is forward-looking arithmetic on a product that has not yet sold a unit at scale.

Electric Motorcycles segment operating expense runs approximately $62 million a year. For that segment to reach operating breakeven on Honcho gross profit alone requires between 46,000 units (at a 31% margin, the 2021 long-term target) and 103,000 units (at 14%).

The Honcho is a change in revenue mix, not a change in solvency.

At an optimistic 3,000 units and a 31% gross margin, the Honcho contributes roughly $4 million of gross profit against $62 million of segment operating expense — about 6% of overhead. It enters the segment 39% above the category leader's price, with no established nameplate, 105 dealers, and an electric powertrain carrying range and charging friction a $3,599 gasoline Grom does not have.

Note also what has not changed. The 2021 plan promised ~100,000 units. After cutting cost per unit 47%, headcount 18%, and R&D from $54.1M to $23.9M, the volume required to break even is still roughly the 2021 plan number. The cost reductions were real. They did not move the bar.

How many Honchos keep the company afloat?

“Afloat” is a cash question, not an accounting one. LiveWire consumed $29.9 million in the first half of 2026 — call it $60 million a year. That is the hole the Honcho would have to fill on its own.

Contribution per unit is the variable. At a recognised wholesale price near $4,300, a KYMCO-built machine plausibly returns $500 to $1,500 per unit after landed cost, freight, warranty and dealer support.

619 Honchos per dealer, per year. Twelve a week, every week, at every LiveWire dealer.

That is what 65,000 units divided by 105 dealers looks like. A typical powersports dealership sells a few hundred motorcycles a year across every brand and category it carries. And 65,000 units at $4,300 recognised is $280 million of revenue — nine times the company's current total.

Run it the other way and the picture is clearer still. At a realistic 3,000 units, the Honcho contributes about $2.7 million against $60 million of annual burn: 4.5% of the hole.

The likely case: roughly 2,000 units

Set aside the breakeven tables for a moment and take the realistic number. On the segment evidence — a category leader selling about 10,000 a year with Honda's network, a new nameplate at a 39% price premium, 105 dealers, and an electric powertrain in a segment that buys on price — roughly 2,000 Honchos globally in the first full year is a defensible estimate. It may be generous.

Basis for the 2,000-unit estimate

This figure is the author's, and it rests on direct working experience with the data rather than on a model. As Sales Planning Lead for Honda's CRF line, the author worked with Motorcycle Industry Council registration volumes as a primary planning input — the same dataset that governs how U.S. two-wheel share is actually counted, and which is not publicly available. Product planning at Harley-Davidson across the Touring, CVO and Trike lines supplied the second half: what a dealer network of a given size can physically retail in a season.

The estimate reflects three things that dataset makes plain. The minimoto segment is small and concentrated — the Grom is not the leader, it is very nearly the whole category. New nameplates without an established service and parts footprint take years, not quarters, to register meaningful volume. And 105 retail points cannot move what 1,000 can, regardless of the product.

The estimate is not load-bearing. It is offered because a specific number is more useful than a vague one, and because the conclusion holds across a wide range: nothing below roughly 40,000 units changes any finding in this brief. A reader who thinks the Honcho will do 8,000 arrives at the same place.

At that volume the Honcho does not merely fail to help. It costs money in three separate ways.

At ~2,000 unitsConsequence
1. It contributes almost nothing At $900 of contribution per unit, roughly $1.8 million — 3.0% of annual cash burn and 1.5% of the $121.8M that must be found by December 2027. Revenue of about $8.6 million against a company that needs orders of magnitude more.
2. It probably triggers the KYMCO deficit fee The KYMCO agreement obliges LiveWire to order at least the Minimum Annual Volume Commitment for each product, or pay an Order Deficit Impact Fee. The committed volumes are redacted — but a manufacturer tooling a new platform does not set them at 2,000. Selling well below commitment converts a growth product into a fixed liability.
3. It drops H-D below the 40% floor On the units reading of “production”: about 900 H-D-built motorcycles against 2,000 KYMCO-built ones is 31%. The threshold is crossed at roughly 1,350 non-H-D units. A modest Honcho success trips it; only failure keeps LiveWire compliant.
And the failure mode is already on the record — twice

Faced with a volume shortfall, the KYMCO agreement gives LiveWire a choice: pay the Order Deficit Impact Fee, or take receipt of the units needed to meet the commitment. Neither branch is hypothetical.

What buying motorcycles does. Under the Harley-Davidson agreement, H-D builds and LiveWire purchases the motorcycles into its own inventory at cost-plus. On arrival they are written down to net realisable value. Roughly 87% of Q2 2026's gross loss was that write-down. The FY2025 10-K states the relationship without varnish, explaining why costs fell that year: “primarily driven by a significant decrease of the number of motorcycles purchased in 2025 as compared to 2024 resulting in lower net realizable value adjustments, as well as the effect of motorcycles that were purchased in 2024 and sold in 2025 that were already written down to net realizable value.” Margins improved because the company bought fewer motorcycles. Volume is what hurts.

What not buying them does. In 2023 LiveWire recorded a $6,126 thousand provision for a liability for excess inventory components held by H-D under the terms of the Contract Manufacturing Agreement — parts Harley-Davidson had procured to build motorcycles LiveWire did not take. That is the take-or-pay exposure operating four years before the formal per-product minimums begin.

So the calendar-2027 commitments do not introduce a new risk. They formalise one that has already cost $6.1 million once, on a contract whose transfer price exceeds the product's market value — and the same structure now exists in duplicate with KYMCO.

Note on terminology: no motorcycles have been repurchased from dealers. Retail sell-through exceeded wholesale shipments in both Q2 and H1 2026. The write-downs are on inventory LiveWire owns, not returned stock.

Dust does not change this on any near timetable. The assets were acquired in May 2026; the Hightail was still in final validation with assembly in Bend, Oregon. Integration, tooling, homologation and dealer placement put meaningful volume in 2027 at the earliest, and off-road electric is not a high-volume category at that price. The one consolation is symmetrical: consideration is up to $14.75M but mostly contingent and payable in stock, so a programme that never scales also never pays out.

A product named once, in a recital

The Covered Product is identified in a single WHEREAS clause: “a Memorandum of Understanding on or about 17 October 2024 (the ‘Parisian MOU’) related to the joint design and manufacture of an EV maxi-scooter (‘Parisian’).”

The word “scooter” appears twice in the entire agreement, both inside that one sentence. Everything after it runs on the defined term “Covered Product,” which appears 24 times. A reader who skips the recitals never learns what the product is — and no LiveWire filing, release or presentation names it anywhere else.

One distinction the analysis should keep straight: the Covered Product carrying the five-year exclusivity is the Parisian. The S4 Honcho, if it appears at all, is a Product under the redacted Exhibit A — a weaker category. The minimum volume commitment in §2.3 attaches to “each Product,” so it very likely reaches the Honcho; the exclusivity architecture, however, was built around a scooter that has never been announced.

A correction to earlier revisions of this brief

Previous drafts treated the 40% clause as the constraint governing how much production LiveWire could move to KYMCO — and suggested it capped the affordability strategy at roughly a thousand units a year. Reading the agreement, that overstates it.

Section 8.4 lets either party terminate the entire Master Services Agreement for convenience on ninety days' notice. Harley-Davidson does not need the 40% trigger; it needs no reason at all, and convenience termination is faster than the 180-day route. The 40% clause is the narrower and slower of the two paths.

What the clause is genuinely evidence of is intent: in 2022, and again when the parties re-papered the agreement in December 2024, both sides contemplated that LiveWire might move manufacturing away from Harley-Davidson, and Harley-Davidson wanted a documented trigger tied to it. That is worth recording. It is not a cap on the Honcho.

And “production” is not a defined term. The word appears exactly once in the agreement, lowercase, inside Section 8.3(c). There is no capitalised definition of “Production” anywhere in the document. A termination right turns on a word the contract never defines.

Author's note, offered as industry interpretation rather than contract text: in Harley-Davidson's internal product planning vernacular, “production” means motorcycles built at the plant — unit output, not revenue and not platforms. On that reading the test is a unit-count ratio of H-D-built motorcycles to LiveWire's total motorcycle production, and STACYC balance bikes fall outside it. That is the most natural reading of an undefined term, but it is an interpretation, and the agreement does not supply it.

Nor does cutting solve it

The obvious counter is to shrink the company to fit the product. Run the arithmetic: at 3,000 Honchos and $900 of contribution, plus roughly 1,000 S2 units at around breakeven and STACYC's small operating income, total operating expense would need to fall to under about $5 million a year to reach cash neutrality. Electric Motorcycles segment operating expense is currently $62 million.

That is not a cost-reduction programme. It is a description of a much smaller company that no longer designs motorcycles — and it would not service the $76.8 million due in December 2027 either.

The conclusion follows from the arithmetic rather than from any judgement about the product: there is no combination of Honcho volume and cost reduction that reaches solvency at a scale this category can supply. Which is why the resolution, when it comes, will be financial rather than commercial.

The at-the-market programme is the only other lever on the balance sheet, and it is not one — for a reason buried in the loan agreement. Beyond that, $48.4 million remains undrawn, covering under 40% of the gap even if fully placed — and placing it means issuing roughly $48 million of stock into a float of about 44 million shares at $1.20, which is not a financing so much as a description of what would happen to the share price.

What the number is actually telling you

No one at LiveWire or Harley-Davidson expects this note to be repaid out of motorcycle sales, and this brief does not suggest anyone believes otherwise. The arithmetic is worth doing precisely because the answer is unreachable: it establishes that the December 2027 maturity will be settled by a financial decision rather than a commercial one.

Harley-Davidson holds both the secured note and roughly 78% of the equity. It is therefore on both sides of that decision, and the range of outcomes — extension, conversion, exchange, sale, or something else — is largely its to choose. No filing states what it intends, and none is asserted here.

09 — The other side

What LiveWire earned

An analysis that only prosecutes is not an analysis. The following is real, and mostly unreported.

  • Cost per motorcycle nearly halved — $44,147 in 2024 to $23,172 in Q2 2026. That is genuine engineering and sourcing work.
  • Loss per unit down 61% in two years.
  • Real spending discipline. R&D fell from $54.1M (2023) to $23.9M (2025). Headcount 182 to approximately 150.
  • STACYC is profitable. Q2 2026: revenue $5,469K, gross profit $2,495K — a 45.6% gross margin and positive operating income.
  • Operating cash burn improved 18.4% — net cash used by operating activities fell from $32.4M in H1 2025 to $26.4M in H1 2026.
  • Retail sell-through grew 171% in Q2, and exceeded wholesale shipments in both Q2 and H1. Whatever else is true, this was not channel stuffing. The demand is real, at its scale.
  • Dealer count is recovering — 88 at the end of 2024, 96 at the end of 2025, 105 at 30 June 2026.

The executives who made the 2021 projections are not the people running the company today. The trajectory on cost is right. The level is unsurvivable, and the funding window closed in February 2026.

10 — Analysis, not reporting

What a financial resolution could look like

Read this section differently

Every preceding section is sourced to a filing, a company release, or a manufacturer's published price. This one is not. It sets out mechanisms available to the parties under instruments already on file. It assigns no probabilities, and it states no intention on anyone's behalf.

No filing indicates that Harley-Davidson intends to divest, wind down, convert, acquire, or take private its interest in LiveWire, and nothing below should be read as suggesting one has been chosen. The closing outlook is the author's judgment, identified as such.

The repositioning, in dated instruments

Read on its own, each of the following is routine corporate housekeeping. Read in sequence, they describe one thing.

DateInstrument or statement
14 Feb 2024H-D extends a $100M Convertible Delayed Draw Term Loan. If LiveWire cannot repay at maturity, H-D converts to equity. That is a parent's instrument — an equity backstop. It is never drawn.
9 Nov 2025Amended and restated. The conversion feature is removed. A security interest over substantially all assets is added. So are negative covenants blocking asset sales, new debt, liens, investments and fundamental changes. And the first $10.0M of any ATM proceeds is redirected to the lender.
15 Dec 2025$75.0M drawn. Interest accrues at SOFR + 4.00%, compounding semi-annually, nothing payable until maturity.
26 Feb 2026H-D's audited 10-K: “does not plan to make additional investments in LiveWire beyond the amount outstanding under the Term Loan.”
5 May 2026“Back to the Bricks.” Six financial targets, none of them LiveWire. The word “electric” does not appear.

In fifteen months, Harley-Davidson moved from parent to secured creditor.

Every step above is a filed instrument with a date on it. What the sequence means is interpretation; that it occurred is not. And the direction is consistent: an equity backstop was replaced with a secured claim, the borrower's ability to sell anything or restructure was constrained, and the only equity-raising mechanism available to the company was made to pay down the lender first.

One thing the author does not claim to know

Why the conversion right was removed. Two readings fit the same facts. A lender expecting to be repaid or refinanced has no use for a conversion feature and would reasonably drop it. A lender who would rather hold a secured claim over the assets than a diluted stake in the equity would drop it for the opposite reason.

The distinction matters — it is roughly the difference between an extension and a foreclosure — and nothing on file settles it. Any brief claiming to know which is guessing.

One structural fact drives the whole set. Harley-Davidson holds both the secured note and roughly 78% of the equity. In an ordinary distressed situation a creditor negotiates with shareholders; here they are the same party. The only outside interest with anything at stake is the ~22% public float.

MechanismHow it worksWhat it costs H-D
Extend or amend the note Maturity pushed beyond December 2027, possibly repriced or re-covenanted. Threads the “no additional investments” language, since extending an existing note is not new investment. Nothing today. Preserves every other option.
Convert debt to equity Correction worth noting: H-D used to hold this as a contractual right. The February 2024 Convertible Delayed Draw Term Loan let H-D convert amounts outstanding to equity at maturity if it determined, reasonably and in good faith, that LiveWire lacked the financial wherewithal to repay. That facility was never drawn, and the November 2025 amendment and restatement removed the conversion feature. A conversion today would require a fresh negotiated agreement, not the exercise of an existing right. The arithmetic if it happened: at $1.20, $85M is roughly 71 million shares, taking H-D from ~78% toward ~88%. Delaware's short-form merger provision becomes available at 90% — above which a parent may squeeze out minority holders without a shareholder vote. No cash. Dilutes the minority substantially.
Harley's consent right over all of it The Term Loan carries negative covenants restricting LiveWire's ability to incur indebtedness, create liens, sell assets, make investments, make fundamental changes, pay dividends, or enter into affiliate transactions. Selling STACYC, raising senior money, or merging with anyone plausibly requires the lender's agreement. Nothing. It already holds it.
Take-private Acquire the float. Requires a Schedule 13E-3, a special committee of independent directors, and a fairness opinion — the function the conflicts committee described on the 2021 investor call was created to perform. ~$54M at market, and minority squeeze-outs typically carry a 30–50% premium. Less than another year of funding.
Sale to a third party KYMCO is the structurally obvious counterparty — existing shareholder, manufacturer of the volume product, short on Western brand and distribution. Complicated by the secured note sitting ahead of all other claims. Recovers something; crystallises the loss.
Break-up STACYC is separable and profitable — 45.6% gross margin and positive operating income in Q2 2026. It would sell readily. Selling it also removes the only profitable segment and makes the remainder strictly worse. Raises cash. If STACYC is sold, the remainder is being prepared for wind-down, not growth.
Outside capital Named directly in H-D's own risk factor: “the ability of LiveWire to obtain sufficient funding from sources other than the Company.” A new investor would face a $75M annual loss, a secured creditor ahead of them, and a 78% controlling holder above them — and would likely require H-D to subordinate or convert first. Nothing, but routes back to conversion.
Secured-creditor remedies The note is secured by substantially all assets. On default, foreclosure — or a reorganisation with H-D as debtor-in-possession lender and stalking-horse bidder — transfers the assets and leaves the public equity behind. A documented legal right, recorded here for completeness. Recovers assets. Wipes out the minority.
Delisting Compliance is fragile: the shares were non-compliant with NYSE Section 802.01C as recently as July 2026 and trade near $1.20 today. A move off the exchange reduces the cost of being public and destroys what the 2022 business combination was for. Nothing. Passive.

The incentive that probably sets the timing

Every financial target in “Back to the Bricks” is an HDMC target — EBITDA above $350M by 2027, 25–30% gross margin, operating expense under 20% of sales. LiveWire's $70–80 million annual drag does not appear in those metrics, but it does appear in Harley-Davidson's consolidated results. Anything that deconsolidates LiveWire improves the reported picture at the cost of crystallising the loss. That tension — reported earnings against a one-time write-off — is the most plausible driver of when, whatever the what turns out to be.

The Harley-Davidson read-through

LiveWire is a $247 million company with a 44-million-share float. Harley-Davidson is a $27.81 stock that trades. For most readers the more consequential question is not what happens to LiveWire — it is what LiveWire is doing to the parent's reported numbers, and what changes if it stops.

Harley-Davidson consolidates LiveWire, and reports it as one of three segments alongside HDMC and HDFS. That means LiveWire's losses run through H-D's income statement, reduced only by the share attributed to LiveWire's minority holders.

An anomaly worth flagging — and not explaining away

Harley-Davidson owns roughly 78% of LiveWire. On a straight ownership split, the minority would absorb about 22% of the loss — some $4.0 million in the quarter. The filed figure is $824 thousand, or 4.5%.

So Harley-Davidson is bearing about 95% of an economic loss in a company it owns 78% of. There are ordinary accounting explanations — loss attribution once minority equity is exhausted, basis differences, the elimination of intercompany manufacturing margin — and this brief does not claim to know which applies. The figures are stated because both are filed and the gap between them is large. Determining the cause requires H-D's equity roll-forward and is beyond this brief's scope.

On the half year the effect is larger. LiveWire's H1 net loss was $36.3 million; $1.5 million went to minority interests. Removing the remainder would lift H-D's net income attributable from $104.6M to $139.4M — a third higher — and first-half diluted EPS from $0.97 to roughly $1.33. Annualised, LiveWire is worth on the order of $0.65 of Harley-Davidson EPS.

What that arithmetic does not say

Removing a loss is not the same as deconsolidating a business. Three things sit between the calculation above and any real outcome, and all three cut against it.

Harley-Davidson earns money from LiveWire. It manufactures the motorcycles on a cost-plus basis with a mark-up, charges a monthly services fee, finances the dealers through HDFS, and now collects interest at SOFR + 4%. Those revenues disappear with the customer. The net benefit of separation is smaller than the loss it removes — possibly much smaller.

Deconsolidation would crystallise a charge. H-D carries an investment in LiveWire and a $76.8 million secured receivable. What comes off the balance sheet, and at what value, is not derivable from LiveWire's filings — it requires Harley-Davidson's own carrying value, which this brief has not established.

And the targets already exclude it. Every financial target in “Back to the Bricks” is an HDMC target — EBITDA above $350M by 2027, 25–30% gross margin, operating expense under 20% of sales. LiveWire never appears in the metrics management is measured against, but it does appear in the consolidated results shareholders are paid on. That gap is the whole tension.

Which reframes the question for anyone holding HOG rather than LVWR. Harley-Davidson is currently absorbing roughly $70 million a year of losses, and about $0.65 of annual EPS, from a segment that is absent from its own five-year plan and whose funding it has publicly capped. The strategic decision has already been made and disclosed. The accounting has not caught up to it. When it does — by sale, deconsolidation, restructuring or write-down — the reported numbers move, in one direction on earnings and possibly the other on a one-time charge.

Ground Truth No. 02 will take up the parent directly: Harley-Davidson's carrying value of LiveWire, the write-off arithmetic, and whether the Back to the Bricks targets are reachable on HDMC alone.

Outlook — the author's judgment

The commercial question is already settled, and it was settled by arithmetic rather than by execution. Clearing December 2027 on product requires roughly 135,000 Honchos in about fourteen months, in a segment whose best-selling machine does 10,000 a year with Honda's dealer network behind it. No plausible ramp closes that. The Honcho may well be a good motorcycle; it cannot be a solvency event.

At the realistic figure — on the order of 2,000 units globally — the position is worse than neutral. The Honcho would contribute about 3% of annual cash burn, probably incur a deficit fee against a minimum volume commitment it cannot meet, and drop Harley-Davidson below the 40% manufacturing floor. The distance between the volume that rescues the company and the volume the category can supply is not a difference of degree. Nothing below roughly 40,000 units changes a single conclusion in this brief.

The financial question is therefore the only live one, and one party decides it. Harley-Davidson holds the note, the security interest over substantially all assets, roughly 78% of the equity, the board, the covenants restricting asset sales and fundamental changes, the manufacturing, the dealer financing through HDFS, the building, and a services agreement it can cancel for convenience on ninety days' notice. There is no counterparty with leverage, and the public float has none whatsoever.

Timing points to the first half of 2027. The 10-Q asserts roughly twelve months of runway from issuance — about August 2027 — and the note matures 15 December 2027. Those converge. Something forces the question earlier still: the FY2026 10-K lands around February 2027, and an auditor must take a going-concern view with nine months of cash against $85 million coming due.

Base case: extension or restructuring on Harley-Davidson's terms. It is the cheapest path, it preserves every other option, it produces no write-off headline, and Harley controls both sides of the table. The tell would be an 8-K amending the credit agreement.

What would be genuinely surprising is outside capital. The security interest and the covenants make LiveWire close to unfundable by anyone willing to rank behind Harley-Davidson — which is the practical meaning of the risk factor about obtaining funding “from sources other than the Company.”

A complication that is not yet priced anywhere. Four vehicle programmes are now under way — the S2 platform, the S4 Honcho, the acquired Dust line, and the undisclosed Parisian maxi-scooter — carrying minimum-volume commitments to two separate manufacturers, on a $23.9M annual R&D budget and roughly 150 people. That is a great deal of committed forward spend for a company whose funding was capped in February 2026.

What this brief does not conclude

That LiveWire fails. A controlled restructuring by a parent that owns 78% of the equity is not a failure event for the business; it is a change in who owns it and on what terms. The people running the company cut cost per motorcycle 47% in two years, improved operating cash burn 18.4% year over year, run a profitable STACYC segment, and rebuilt a dealer network they had cut by 30%. That is execution.

What the filings describe is not a company being run badly. It is a company whose structural position was fixed in place by the end of 2025, and whose remaining decisions belong to someone else.

This brief does not assert, and no filing states, that Harley-Davidson intends to divest, wind down, or take LiveWire private. No Schedule 13D has ever been filed on LiveWire. Harley-Davidson's sole ownership filing is a Form 3 dated 26 September 2022, filed jointly by ElectricSoul, LLC, Harley-Davidson Motor Company Group, LLC and Harley-Davidson, Inc.

The Master Services Agreement of 23 December 2024 has now been read. “Production” is not a defined term in it — the word appears once, lowercase, in Section 8.3(c). The reading offered in §07 is the author's industry interpretation of an undefined term, identified as such, and is not represented as the agreement's own definition.
