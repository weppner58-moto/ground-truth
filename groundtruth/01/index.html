00 — Why this brief exists

Who I am, and why I wrote this

A percentage got my attention. The contracts kept it.

LiveWire's marketing lead put up a post about 386% growth in Q2. The number is accurate, and on its own it would be worth a paragraph — big percentage on a tiny base is the oldest move in the book and anyone who has sat in a planning meeting has watched it get built. What turned it into two weeks of reading was what was sitting underneath: two contract manufacturing agreements signed the same day, which together make buying bikes value-destroying and not buying them expensive. A services agreement Harley-Davidson can cancel on ninety days’ notice for no reason at all. A loan that got its conversion feature quietly removed and a lien over basically everything put in its place. And a fourth vehicle programme, under contract for two years, that shows up in exactly zero narrative disclosures.

None of that is in the earnings release. None of it has been reported. It lives in the exhibits — the attachments to the filings that nobody opens, including most of the people who cover the stock.

I started with LiveWire because it is the only pure-play electric motorcycle company in the United States that has to file with the SEC. Everyone else in this category — Zero, Stark, Sur-Ron, Dust until it got bought — is private. Private means you get the numbers they feel like giving you. LiveWire has to file audited statements, attach its contracts and put the figures on the record whether they flatter it or not. That makes it the one place in this business where the press release can be checked against the arithmetic.

And the gap is the whole story. A quarter goes out as 386% growth; the filing shows 923 motorcycles in twelve months. A 76% market share gets claimed; three straight annual reports never say share of what. A new product is sold as the turnaround; the manufacturing agreement behind it commits the company to buy volume it has no way to move. None of it is hidden. It is just in documents most people never open.

That is the hurdle I want to clear. Suppliers deciding whether to tool up for this category. Investors sizing a position. Dealers weighing a franchise. Engineers deciding where to put five years. Buyers wondering whether the company behind a $15,000 motorcycle will exist to honour the warranty. All of them are working off press releases, because the primary documents are long, dull, and written to be technically true rather than clear.

So I read them. All 260 filings for both companies, plus the manufacturing agreements, the loan documents and the exhibits nobody quotes. Then I show the work, link every figure to where it came from, and publish my corrections when I get something wrong. This is not an attack on LiveWire. It is an attempt to make the electric motorcycle market legible to people who have to make decisions about it — and to give them the same footing the manufacturers already have.

A product that appears in no filing except one exhibit

The Amended and Restated Contract Manufacturing Agreement filed 22 May 2026 is with KYMCO, not Harley-Davidson. Its recitals name the covered product: “LiveWire’s Parisian maxi-scooter.” The two sides signed a memorandum of understanding on it dated 17 October 2024, for the joint design and manufacture of an EV maxi-scooter.

The words “Parisian” and “maxi-scooter” appear nowhere else in any LiveWire filing. Not a 10-K, not a 10-Q, not a press release. A fourth vehicle programme, under contract for nearly two years, exists in the public record only in the preamble of an exhibit. I have sat in enough product-plan reviews to know what a fourth platform costs a 150-person company. Nobody has been told it exists.

The agreement also puts LiveWire on the hook for a Minimum Annual Volume Commitment on each product, enforced by an Order Deficit Impact Fee: miss the number and you either take delivery of the shortfall or pay for it anyway. Supplier people will recognise that as take-or-pay. It sits alongside the per-product minimums owed to Harley-Davidson from calendar 2027. Every pivot moved the price. None of them moved the overhead.

Two things fall out of that. First, the S2 is now selling at roughly the realised average the segment reports — $11,999 sticker against $11,483 recognised — which is what makes the net realisable value write-downs make sense: the cost-plus transfer price coming out of Harley-Davidson sits above where the market will clear the bike. Anyone who has run a transfer-price negotiation between an OEM and its own sub will know how that conversation goes.

Second, the Honcho is not another rung on this ladder. It is a different business. $4,999, built by KYMCO instead of Harley-Davidson, sold into the minimoto segment instead of the premium roadster segment — it shares a badge with the S2 and not much else. Dust points the same way: off-road, light, cheaper to homologate. Put those next to STACYC, the only segment that makes money, and the portfolio now describes a retreat from the premium on-road thesis that justified a $2.31 billion valuation.

Left to right, this is a company that vacated the top of its own map. The LiveWire ONE, once $29,799, now sits at $13,999 and is the most expensive thing the brand sells. Can-Am — which bought the Alta patents — brackets the S2 line from below on both the road and dual-sport rows.

And the Honcho does not land in open space. It lands inside the e-moto band, on top of Sur-Ron and Talaria machines that already own that price, while sitting $1,400 above the combustion Grom that outsells everything in the category. The one uncontested position on the map is the gap between $6,000 and $10,000 — which is where nobody, LiveWire included, currently builds anything.

Why this belongs in the file

The pattern isn’t that Harley killed Alta — Alta was a venture-funded startup with its own problems and companies fail for a hundred reasons. The pattern is narrower and better documented: Harley-Davidson’s decision to stop funding was, both times, the event that decided what happened next.

In 2018 that decision landed six months after the investment and Alta closed within weeks. In February 2026 it landed in an audited annual report: “The Company does not plan to make additional investments in LiveWire beyond the amount outstanding under the Term Loan.”

The difference this time is that it is a filed statement in a public company’s 10-K, with a maturity date attached and a security interest over substantially all of the borrower’s assets.

There is a second-order irony worth writing down. The Alta patents went to BRP, which now competes in the category. The Silicon Valley R&D centre Harley chose to build instead became part of the LiveWire business whose R&D spend has since dropped from $54.1 million to $23.9 million a year. Eight years and roughly $394 million of cumulative losses later, the electric motorcycle assets Harley-Davidson declined to buy are being sold by a competitor, and the ones it built are not named in its own five-year plan.

05 — Unit economics

Where the money actually goes

In Q2 2026 the Electric Motorcycles segment recognised $13,655 per motorcycle in total segment revenue — $11,483 of it the bike itself, the rest parts, accessories and apparel. Cost of goods sold was $23,172 per unit. Selling, admin and engineering was $58,049 per unit. Read those three numbers again. That is the business.

What the write-down actually means

A net realisable value adjustment means LiveWire bought S2s from Harley-Davidson and immediately marked them down to what they can actually be sold for. Under the Contract Manufacturing Agreement those bikes are priced cost-plus — Harley’s cost, plus a mark-up.

The write-down is arithmetic proof that the transfer price is above what the motorcycle is worth on a showroom floor. The loss is real; it just gets booked at purchase instead of at sale, and it comes out of the contract, not the plant.

Three qualifications, stated before anyone else states them

The base is the trough. Q2 2025’s 55 units is what makes 386% look impressive and what makes the flat loss look damning. Anyone using that quarter as a base — in either direction — picked a flattering comparison, and I include myself in that.

LiveWire has a legitimate explanation and it is in the same filing. Operating expense fell $967 thousand. Personnel came down $1,728 thousand on lower headcount; that saving got eaten by S4 Honcho development spend plus the costs of the Dust deal and the ATM programme. Reinvesting a cost-out into the next product is what a functioning company does. I have signed off on that trade-off myself.

The annual trend runs the other way, and it’s real. See below.

The other number: 76% market share

The same post claimed 76% U.S. market share. Three things about that.

On the source of that figure

The most likely source is Motorcycle Industry Council registration data, which is the standard basis for U.S. two-wheel share and the thing every planning office in this industry lives on. MIC data is not public. It is a paid product that goes to reporting manufacturers and subscribers, and the model-level detail underneath it is available to OEMs and licensees, not to readers.

That has a direct consequence for this brief and for anyone reading the claim: the specifics cannot be independently verified. Not because the number is necessarily wrong — because the dataset it almost certainly rests on is one you cannot buy your way into, and LiveWire has published no definition next to it. So this section looks at what can be checked — whether a definition exists anywhere in the filings, and which measure the company itself nominates — and stops there.

First, the segment isn’t defined anywhere in the filings. The words “kilowatt” and “horsepower” do not appear once in LiveWire’s FY2025 Form 10-K. Whatever denominator produces 76% is a marketing construction with no disclosed basis in the company’s SEC reporting — which means it can’t be checked, and a share figure that can’t be checked isn’t a share figure.

That absence holds across three consecutive annual reports. FY2023, FY2024 and FY2025: no segment definition, no power threshold, and no mention of the Motorcycle Industry Council.

How a share figure of that size gets built — author's note

U.S. two-wheel share is generally measured from registration data the MIC compiles from manufacturer reporting. There is a structural feature of that dataset worth understanding if you have never worked with it: segment classification is declared by the manufacturer. Where a given model sits — dual-sport or enduro, standard or cruiser, on-road or off — is a decision the maker submits, not one an independent body assigns.

In product planning at Honda and at Harley-Davidson, my direct experience is that classification is treated as exactly what it is: a positioning decision, weighed with its effect on your reported competitive position in view. That is an ordinary, well-understood part of how the industry uses this data. It is also why a share percentage drawn from it means nothing without the segment definition stapled to it.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Restrict a segment to on-road electric motorcycles above a power threshold and the U.S. competitive set narrows to a handful of nameplates. Set that threshold at 50 kilowatts and machines making 47 horsepower — the Can-Am Pulse and Origin, the most significant recent entrants — fall outside it entirely, while LiveWire’s own 63 kW and 75 kW products stay inside. The threshold, not the performance, decides who gets counted.

None of this is a claim about what LiveWire did. The company has published no definition, so no claim can be made either way. The point is narrower and it is enough: a share figure with an undisclosed denominator cannot be verified, and the mechanism by which such a figure gets made large is standard industry practice.

One day, two filings

The 386% quarter went out on 23 July 2026, in an 8-K under Item 2.02.

Same day, LiveWire got a letter from the New York Stock Exchange saying it was out of compliance with Section 802.01C — as of the 22 July close, the average price of its stock had been under $1.00 for thirty straight trading days. That letter was disclosed six days later, in a separate 8-K under Item 3.01.

The sequence, in the company's own filings

23 July — Q2 earnings release: unit sales up 386%. Same day: NYSE non-compliance notice received.
29 July — the delisting notice is disclosed by 8-K.
31 July — month end, and the first date on which the Exchange’s cure test can be applied. The stock closes at $1.81.
4 August — the board doubles executive severance and carves an H-D acquisition of 100% of the stock out of the definition of “change in control.”
5 August — the 10-Q is filed.

The stock roughly doubled on the earnings news, and then kept going — from a $0.77 close on the day of the release to an intraday $3.57 on 27 July, on 75.6 million shares against a normal day of under 200,000. Under Section 802.01C the price condition is cured if, on the last trading day of a calendar month, both the closing price and the preceding thirty-trading-day average are at or above $1.00. At the 31 July close of $1.81 the stock appears to have cleared it — nine days after the letter. The 386% headline is, quite literally, what got the company off the delisting track.

What is not filed: LiveWire has announced no return to compliance, and the 10-Q filed on 5 August doesn’t mention the letter at all. The cure reading above is mine, worked from the Exchange’s published standard and market closes; it is not a company statement. On the filings alone, the six-month cure period runs to 23 January 2027.

What this is, and what it is not

What the filings actually support is narrow and it is this: on the first days in more than a month when the stock could be sold above $1.00, four insiders sold into that liquidity, none of them under a pre-set plan, and the largest sale was executed by the Chief Executive on the same day the company disclosed a delisting letter it had received six days earlier. The filings don’t establish the order of events inside 29 July, and I don’t assert one. I don’t need to: by 29 July the stock was near $2.40, and the condition the letter described — a price under $1.00 — had visibly stopped being true days earlier. As undisclosed bad news, the letter was already stale.

What the vesting dates do kill is the most comfortable explanation. These were not shares liquidated on receipt as comp converts to cash. They were positions held for weeks, months, and in one case more than two years, and sold in the single week the stock traded above $2.00 — before or since. That is not evidence of anything improper. It is evidence of price sensitivity: four insiders looked at $1.98 to $2.38, on a stock that had spent the previous month under a dollar, and took it. It closed at $1.16 on 3 September.

The balancing fact is real and it belongs here: all four are still heavily long. Mr Donnez’s own Form 4 shows 1,344,076 unvested restricted stock units still outstanding — roughly six times what he sold. Mr Nienhuis shows 110,608 unvested; Mr Gruner 88,029. Nobody exited. What they did was take a slice of a liquidity window that hadn’t existed the week before, and it was created by the number this brief opened with.

Electric Motorcycles segment operating expense runs about $62 million a year. For that segment to get to operating breakeven on Honcho gross profit alone, you need somewhere between 46,000 units (at a 31% margin — the 2021 long-term target) and 103,000 units (at 14%).

The Honcho is a change in revenue mix, not a change in solvency.

At an optimistic 3,000 units and a 31% gross margin, the Honcho throws off roughly $4 million of gross profit against $62 million of segment operating expense — about 6% of the overhead. It walks into the segment 39% above the category leader’s price, with no nameplate, 105 dealers, and an electric powertrain carrying range and charging friction that a $3,599 gasoline Grom simply doesn’t have.

Note what has not changed. The 2021 plan promised ~100,000 units. After cutting cost per unit 47%, headcount 18%, and R&D from $54.1M to $23.9M, the volume needed to break even is still roughly the 2021 plan number. The cost-out was real. It didn’t move the bar.

Basis for the 2,000-unit estimate

This number is mine, and it rests on having done the job rather than on a model. I held planning lead responsibility for the Honda Grom — the bike that defines this segment — and, as Sales Planning Lead for Honda’s CRF line, worked with Motorcycle Industry Council registration volumes as a primary planning input. That is the dataset that governs how U.S. two-wheel share is actually counted, and it is not public. Product planning at Harley-Davidson across Touring, CVO and Trike supplied the other half: what a dealer network of a given size can physically retail in a season.

The estimate reflects three things that dataset makes obvious. The minimoto segment is small and concentrated — the Grom isn’t the leader, it is very nearly the whole category. New nameplates without a service and parts footprint take years, not quarters, to register real volume. And 105 retail points cannot move what 1,000 can, no matter how good the bike is.

The estimate is not load-bearing. I give a number because a specific one is more useful than a vague one, and because the conclusion holds across a wide range: nothing under roughly 40,000 units changes any finding in this brief. If you think the Honcho does 8,000, you land in the same place.

At that volume the Honcho doesn’t just fail to help. It costs money in three separate ways.

And the failure mode is already on the record — twice

Faced with a volume shortfall, the KYMCO agreement gives LiveWire a choice: pay the Order Deficit Impact Fee, or take delivery of the units needed to hit the commitment. Neither branch is hypothetical.

What buying motorcycles does. Under the Harley-Davidson agreement, H-D builds and LiveWire buys the bikes into its own inventory at cost-plus. On arrival they get written down to net realisable value. Roughly 87% of Q2 2026’s gross loss was that write-down. The FY2025 10-K says it without varnish, explaining why costs fell that year: “primarily driven by a significant decrease of the number of motorcycles purchased in 2025 as compared to 2024 resulting in lower net realizable value adjustments, as well as the effect of motorcycles that were purchased in 2024 and sold in 2025 that were already written down to net realizable value.” Margins improved because the company bought fewer motorcycles. Volume is what hurts.

What not buying them does. In 2023 LiveWire booked a $6,126 thousand provision for a liability for excess inventory components held by H-D under the terms of the Contract Manufacturing Agreement — parts Harley-Davidson had already bought to build motorcycles LiveWire didn’t take. That is the take-or-pay exposure working four years before the formal per-product minimums kick in.

So the calendar-2027 commitments don’t introduce a new risk. They formalise one that has already cost $6.1 million once, on a contract whose transfer price sits above the product’s market value — and the same structure now exists in duplicate with KYMCO.

Note on terminology, because I got asked: no motorcycles have been bought back from dealers. Retail sell-through beat wholesale in both Q2 and H1 2026. The write-downs are on inventory LiveWire owns, not returned stock.

The one consolation is symmetrical: consideration is up to $14.75M but mostly contingent and payable in stock, so a programme that never scales also never pays out.

A correction to earlier revisions of this brief

Earlier drafts treated the 40% clause as the thing governing how much production LiveWire could move to KYMCO — and suggested it capped the affordability strategy at roughly a thousand units a year. Reading the agreement properly, that overstates it.

Section 8.4 lets either party terminate the entire Master Services Agreement for convenience on ninety days’ notice. Harley-Davidson doesn’t need the 40% trigger; it needs no reason at all, and convenience termination is faster than the 180-day route. The 40% clause is the narrower and slower of the two paths.

What the clause is genuinely evidence of is intent: in 2022, and again when the two sides re-papered the agreement in December 2024, both of them contemplated LiveWire moving manufacturing away from Harley-Davidson, and Harley wanted a documented trigger tied to it. Worth recording. Not a cap on the Honcho.

And “production” is not a defined term. The word appears exactly once in the agreement, lowercase, inside Section 8.3(c). There is no capitalised definition of “Production” anywhere in the document. A termination right turns on a word the contract never defines.

My read, offered as industry interpretation and not contract text: in Harley-Davidson’s internal product-planning vernacular, “production” means motorcycles built at the plant — unit output, not revenue and not platforms. I used the word that way in planning meetings for years. On that reading the test is a unit-count ratio of H-D-built motorcycles to LiveWire’s total motorcycle production, and STACYC balance bikes fall outside it. That is the most natural reading of an undefined term, but it is an interpretation, and the agreement doesn’t supply it.

Nor does cutting solve it

The obvious counter is to shrink the company to fit the product. Run it: at 3,000 Honchos and $900 of contribution, plus roughly 1,000 S2 units at around breakeven and STACYC’s small operating income, total operating expense would have to fall to under about $5 million a year to reach cash neutrality. Electric Motorcycles segment operating expense is currently $62 million.

That is not a cost-reduction programme. That is a description of a much smaller company that no longer designs motorcycles — and it wouldn’t service the $76.8 million due in December 2027 either.

The conclusion falls out of the arithmetic, not out of any opinion about the bike: there is no combination of Honcho volume and cost cuts that reaches solvency at a scale this category can supply. Which is why the resolution, when it comes, will be financial rather than commercial.

10 — Analysis, not reporting

What a financial resolution could look like

Read this section differently

Every section before this one is sourced to a filing, a company release, or a manufacturer’s published price. This one isn’t. It lays out the mechanisms available to the parties under instruments already on file. It assigns no probabilities and it states no intention on anyone’s behalf.

No filing says Harley-Davidson intends to divest, wind down, convert, acquire, or take private its interest in LiveWire, and nothing below should be read as suggesting one of those has been picked. The closing outlook is my judgment, and it says so.

One thing the author does not claim to know

Why the conversion right was removed. Two readings fit the same facts. A lender expecting to be repaid or refinanced has no use for a conversion feature and would reasonably drop it. A lender who would rather hold a secured claim over the assets than a diluted stake in the equity would drop it for the opposite reason.

The distinction matters — it’s roughly the difference between an extension and a foreclosure — and nothing on file settles it. Anyone claiming to know which is guessing.

One structural fact drives the whole set. Harley-Davidson holds both the secured note and roughly 78% of the equity. In an ordinary distressed situation a creditor negotiates with shareholders; here they are the same party. The only outside interest with anything at stake is the ~22% public float.

What that arithmetic does not say

Removing a loss is not the same as deconsolidating a business. Three things sit between the calculation above and any real outcome, and all three cut against it.

Harley-Davidson makes money from LiveWire. It builds the bikes cost-plus with a mark-up, charges a monthly services fee, floors the dealers through HDFS, and now collects interest at SOFR + 4%. Those revenues leave with the customer. The net benefit of separation is smaller than the loss it removes — possibly a lot smaller.

Deconsolidation crystallises a charge. H-D carries an investment in LiveWire and a $76.8 million secured receivable. What comes off the balance sheet, and at what value, isn’t derivable from LiveWire’s filings — it needs Harley-Davidson’s own carrying value, which this brief hasn’t established.

And the targets already exclude it. Every financial target in “Back to the Bricks” is an HDMC target — EBITDA above $350M by 2027, 25–30% gross margin, operating expense under 20% of sales. LiveWire never appears in the metrics management is measured against, but it does appear in the consolidated results shareholders get paid on. That gap is the whole tension.

Which reframes the question for anyone holding HOG rather than LVWR. Harley-Davidson is currently absorbing roughly $70 million a year of losses, and about $0.65 of annual EPS, from a segment that is absent from its own five-year plan and whose funding it has publicly capped. The strategic decision has already been made and disclosed. The accounting hasn’t caught up. When it does — by sale, deconsolidation, restructuring or write-down — the reported numbers move, one way on earnings and possibly the other way on a one-time charge.

Ground Truth No. 02 takes up the parent directly: Harley-Davidson’s carrying value of LiveWire, the write-off arithmetic, and whether the Back to the Bricks targets are reachable on HDMC alone.

Outlook — my judgment

The commercial question is already settled, and arithmetic settled it, not execution. Clearing December 2027 on product needs roughly 135,000 Honchos in about fourteen months, in a segment whose best-selling bike does 10,000 a year with Honda’s dealer network behind it. No plausible ramp closes that. The Honcho may well be a good motorcycle; it cannot be a solvency event.

At the realistic figure — on the order of 2,000 units globally — the position is worse than neutral. The Honcho would contribute about 3% of annual cash burn, probably incur a deficit fee against a minimum it can’t meet, and push Harley-Davidson below the 40% manufacturing floor. The distance between the volume that saves the company and the volume the category can supply isn’t a difference of degree. Nothing below roughly 40,000 units changes a single conclusion in this brief.

So the financial question is the only live one, and one party decides it. Harley-Davidson holds the note, the security interest over substantially all assets, roughly 78% of the equity, the board, the covenants restricting asset sales and fundamental changes, the manufacturing, the dealer financing through HDFS, the building, and a services agreement it can cancel for convenience on ninety days’ notice. There is no counterparty with leverage, and the public float has none at all.

Timing points to the first half of 2027. The 10-Q asserts roughly twelve months of runway from issuance — about August 2027 — and the note matures 15 December 2027. Those converge. Something forces the question earlier still: the FY2026 10-K lands around February 2027, and an auditor has to take a going-concern view with nine months of cash against $85 million coming due.

Base case: extension or restructuring on Harley-Davidson’s terms. It is the cheapest path, it keeps every other option open, it produces no write-off headline, and Harley controls both sides of the table. The tell would be an 8-K amending the credit agreement.

What would genuinely surprise me is outside capital. The security interest and the covenants make LiveWire close to unfundable by anyone willing to rank behind Harley-Davidson — which is the practical meaning of the risk factor about obtaining funding “from sources other than the Company.”

A complication nobody has priced. Four vehicle programmes are now in flight — the S2 platform, the S4 Honcho, the acquired Dust line, and the undisclosed Parisian maxi-scooter — carrying minimum-volume commitments to two separate manufacturers, on a $23.9M annual R&D budget and roughly 150 people. I have watched OEMs with ten times the headcount struggle to carry four platforms. That is a great deal of committed forward spend for a company whose funding was capped in February 2026.

What this brief does not conclude

That LiveWire fails. A controlled restructuring by a parent that owns 78% of the equity isn’t a failure event for the business; it is a change in who owns it and on what terms. The people running the company cut cost per motorcycle 47% in two years, improved operating cash burn 18.4% year over year, run a profitable STACYC segment, and rebuilt a dealer network they had cut by 30%. That is execution, and I have seen far worse from far bigger companies.

What the filings describe is not a company being run badly. It is a company whose structural position was fixed in place by the end of 2025, and whose remaining decisions belong to someone else.
